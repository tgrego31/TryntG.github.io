Asking Better Questions Gets Better Answers

When I get stuck on code, my first thought is usually just to ask someone what I did wrong. Before reading How to Ask Questions the Smart Way, I never really thought about how much the way I ask the question matters.

After reading it, I started to realize that asking for help is actually part of being a good programmer. If I ask a confusing question or leave out important information, then the person helping me has to spend extra time figuring out what I even mean. On the other hand, if I explain what I am trying to do, what went wrong, and what I already tried, it is a lot easier for someone to actually help me.

Raymond explains that people are more willing to answer questions when it is clear that the person asking has already tried to solve the problem on their own. He recommends things like searching online, reading documentation, experimenting, and looking at previous discussions before asking for help.

Looking through Stack Overflow made this idea a lot easier for me to understand.

In the context of a smart question

For my smart question, I chose “Why is conditional processing of a sorted array faster than of an unsorted array?”

View the Stack Overflow question

In the question, the programmer noticed that the same code ran much faster when an array was sorted compared to when the array contained random values. They included the code they were testing, showed the different run times, and explained that they saw similar results in another programming language.

What I think made this a smart question was that the programmer gave enough information for other people to understand the problem without having to guess. They did not just say something like, “Why is my program slow?” They showed what they were doing, what result they expected, and what actually happened.

This connects really well to Raymond's advice. He says that a good question should clearly explain the symptoms, the environment, what the person already tried, and enough information to reproduce the problem.

The responses to the question also showed why asking this way helps.

Instead of people replying with questions like “What does your code look like?” or “What are you trying to do?”, they were able to go straight into the actual reason. The main explanation involved branch prediction and how the processor handles predictable and unpredictable conditions.

I thought that was interesting because the programmer did not just get a quick answer. They got a detailed explanation of what was happening inside the computer.

To me, that shows what Raymond means by asking a smart question. The person answering does not have to waste time trying to understand the problem first. They can spend that time actually explaining the solution.

In the context of a not-so-smart question

For my not-so-smart example, I chose “Quick start learning Python following industrial standards.”

View the Stack Overflow question

This question was different because the person was asking about a lot of things at once. They wanted advice on learning Python, project organization, coding standards, tools, Docker, source control, documentation, and other parts of professional development.

I do not think the person asking was being lazy. They actually listed a lot of things they had already looked into. The problem was that the question was too broad.

There was not really one clear answer that someone could give.

One person might recommend one tool, while another programmer might use something completely different. That makes it hard for the question to have one useful answer.

Raymond talks about this when he says that open-ended questions can feel like a big time commitment to the person answering. He recommends being specific about exactly what kind of help you want.

That is basically what happened with this question. Instead of getting one detailed answer, the question received comments and was eventually closed. It did not lead to the same kind of useful discussion as the first question.

I think this example shows that even if you have done research, you still need to narrow down what you are actually asking.

In the context of asking for programming help

This assignment also made me think about how I ask questions when I am doing my own programming assignments.

There have been a few times in ICS 314 where I thought my code was wrong, but after looking at it again, I realized I did not fully understand what the assignment was asking.

For example, with some of the TypeScript WODs, I have had cases where the program produced an answer I did not expect. My first reaction was to assume there was something wrong with the code. Sometimes, though, the bigger problem was how I understood the instructions.

If I immediately asked someone, “Why doesn't my code work?”, they would probably need a lot more information before they could help me.

A better way to ask would be to explain what input I used, what output I expected, what output I actually got, and what I already tried.

Raymond also points out that when asking about code, it is much better to provide a small example and explain exactly where the result becomes different from what you expected.

That seems simple, but I can see how much easier it would make things for the person trying to help.

In the context of software engineering

Before this assignment, I mostly thought communication skills were separate from programming skills.

Now I think they are connected a lot more than I realized.

Software engineers are going to get stuck. No matter how good someone is at programming, there will probably be times when they need help from another person. If they cannot explain their problem clearly, then it becomes harder for everyone involved.

The smart Stack Overflow question gave people enough information to focus on the actual technical problem. The not-so-smart question was so broad that people could not really give one clear answer.

That difference stood out to me.

It also made me realize that a smart question does not have to sound extremely technical or complicated. It just needs to show that the person has thought about the problem and is giving other people the information they need.

What I learned

The biggest thing I learned from this assignment is that asking a good question can save a lot of time.

Before asking for help, I should try to solve the problem myself, search for similar problems, and make sure I actually understand what I am asking. If I still need help, then I should explain the problem clearly and include what I already tried.

The smart question showed how doing this can lead to really detailed and useful answers.

The not-so-smart question showed the opposite. Even though the person had clearly done some research, the question was so broad that it was difficult for people to answer.

I think this is something I can use outside of Stack Overflow too. Whether I am asking a professor, classmate, coworker, or another programmer for help, asking a better question will probably make it easier for both of us.
